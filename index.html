Machine learning without training the network

Reservoir computing turns the usual approach to neural networks around. You build a large network of randomly connected neurons and never train it. You feed your data in and let it make ripples, and then train only a thin linear layer that reads those ripples out.

Training that layer is a single least-squares solve, not hours of backpropagation, so everything here learns in milliseconds, inside your browser. No server, no GPU and no downloads are involved.

Each demo below takes on a task that's usually given to LSTMs or CNNs.

Why does a random network work?

Feed a signal into a big random recurrent network and every neuron responds with its own nonlinear mixture of the recent input. Some neurons react to the current value, some to what happened a few steps ago, some to products and combinations of the two. The network is a rich, fading echo of the past, which is why these are called echo state networks.

Given enough of those different echoes, whatever you want to compute can usually be written as a weighted sum of them. Finding the weights is linear regression, which has a closed-form answer. There's no gradient descent, no exploding gradients, no learning-rate schedules and no local minima.

A few settings control the reservoir, and every demo lets you change them:

  • Spectral radius: how long echoes last. Near 0 the network forgets quickly. Above about 1 echoes can keep themselves going and turn chaotic.
  • Input scaling: how hard the input pushes the neurons, from near-linear up to strongly saturated.
  • Leak rate: how sluggish the neurons are, which matches the network to slow or fast signals.
  • Ridge λ: how strongly the readout is kept simple, which guards against overfitting.

Because the reservoir is only “some rich dynamical system”, it doesn't have to be software. People have built reservoirs from buckets of water, photonic chips, spintronic oscillators and soft robot bodies. The water demo simulates the first of these.

Where it's weaker

Reservoir computing isn't a replacement for every kind of deep learning. It shines on time series and dynamical systems: small data, fast training and online learning. Its hyperparameters still need tuning. And when only part of a large system can be observed, networks trained with backpropagation often do better (Vlachas et al., 2020). Nobody is building a chatbot out of one.

Further reading

  1. H. Jaeger & H. Haas (2004). Harnessing nonlinearity: predicting chaotic systems and saving energy in wireless communication. Science 304, 78–80. Reported a 2400× accuracy gain on Mackey–Glass prediction.
  2. W. Maass, T. Natschläger & H. Markram (2002). Real-time computing without stable states. Neural Computation 14(11). The “liquid state machine”.
  3. C. Fernando & S. Sojakka (2003). Pattern recognition in a bucket. ECAL 2003, LNCS 2801. A real water tank solved XOR and told “zero” from “one”.
  4. J. Pathak, Z. Lu, B. R. Hunt, M. Girvan & E. Ott (2017). Using machine learning to replicate chaotic attractors and calculate Lyapunov exponents from data. Chaos 27, 121102.
  5. P. R. Vlachas et al. (2020). Backpropagation algorithms and reservoir computing in recurrent neural networks for the forecasting of complex spatiotemporal dynamics. Neural Networks 126, 191–217. With the full state observed, RC beat LSTM/GRU while needing far less training time.
  6. D. J. Gauthier, E. Bollt, A. Griffith & W. A. S. Barbosa (2021). Next generation reservoir computing. Nature Communications 12, 5564.
  7. D. Verstraeten, B. Schrauwen, D. Stroobandt & J. Van Campenhout (2005). Isolated word recognition with the liquid state machine: a case study. Information Processing Letters 95, 521–528.
  8. K. Vandoorne et al. (2014). Experimental demonstration of reservoir computing on a silicon photonics chip. Nature Communications 5, 3541.
  9. M. Lukoševičius (2012). A practical guide to applying echo state networks. Neural Networks: Tricks of the Trade, LNCS 7700. The best place to start if you want to build your own.
